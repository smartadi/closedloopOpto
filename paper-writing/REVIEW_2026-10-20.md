# Referee report

**Manuscript:** "Feedback Control Analysis for Cortical Dynamics in Mice" (39 pp., including 6 main figures, 6 statistical tables, 5 supplementary figures and 1 supplementary table)

*Note: the copy supplied for blind review carries the author list and affiliation on p. 1, and several self-citations in the reference list name an author of this manuscript (p. 32, lines 893-895). Anonymity is not preserved; the editor may wish to know.*

---

## 1. Summary of the work and its main claims

The authors build a real-time closed-loop system that regulates a mesoscale cortical signal in awake, head-fixed mice. Wide-field calcium imaging (GCaMP8s in excitatory neurons) at 35 Hz provides the measurement; a 638 nm galvanometer-steered laser drives an inhibitory opsin (ChrimsonR under DLX2.0, so expressed in forebrain inhibitory interneurons) at a fixed spot in somatosensory cortex; the controlled output is the mean dF/F in a 10 x 10 pixel (0.173 mm) kernel adjacent to the stimulation spot (pp. 3, 18; lines 89-97, 488-495). End-to-end loop latency is at most 47 ms (Fig. 1F).

The paper makes five claims.

1. **The trial-averaged plant is approximately linear and time-invariant.** Mean evoked suppression grows monotonically with laser power (slopes -0.88 to -3.11 %dF/F per mW; R^2 = 0.73-0.97; p. 4, lines 111-114), and a low-order continuous-time transfer function fitted to the amplitude-normalized impulse response predicts held-out trials with pooled median R^2 = 0.85 (Fig. 2C,D; lines 115-123). Fitted poles give a fast "dip" time constant near 0.113 s and a slower, poorly constrained "rebound" (Fig. 2F; lines 129-138).

2. **Single-trial deviations from that average track brain state.** Variability is lowest in the top motion quartile and rises with absolute 2-4 Hz power; the same pattern holds in stimulus-free windows, so the authors interpret state as acting on ongoing activity rather than on the stimulus response (Fig. 2G, Fig. S2, Table 3; lines 143-176).

3. **Closed loop tracks a -5 %dF/F reference better than open loop.** In 15 sessions from 4 mice (1670 trials), the closed- to open-loop RMSE ratio was 0.85 in the transient (0-1 s) and 0.68 in steady state (1-3 s), below one in all 15 sessions, with across-trial variance ratios of 0.65 and 0.54 (Fig. 3, Table 4; lines 184-214).

4. **Residual closed-loop error is brain-state dependent, and feedback partly rejects a measurable disturbance.** Error attenuation improves with movement (-9 % per SD, p = 0.0048) and degrades with absolute 1-4 Hz power (+8 % per SD, p = 0.0014; Fig. 4D, Table 5). A ridge predictor from the contralateral hemisphere estimates each trial's disturbance; the rejection ratio is 0.58 in 11 retained sessions (Fig. 4E-G, Table 6; lines 252-273).

5. **Preview and model-predictive control extend the design.** With a 1 Hz sinusoidal reference in one mouse (3 sessions), feedback lowers error and variance while a 143 ms preview cancels the phase lag (Fig. 5; lines 275-298). In an offline replay of one session, an MPC built on a VARX model of 100 cortical sites plus the laser lowers median per-trial RMSE from 1.46 to 0.88 %dF/F (Fig. 6; lines 299-323).

The framing claim is procedural: characterize the input-output map, verify monotonicity and approximate linearity, tune a PI controller with feedforward, and then read the residual error as a probe of which fluctuations lie outside a fixed linear design (lines 77-81, 429-441).

This is a careful, unusually candid paper on an important and under-served problem, and the Materials and Methods are among the most complete I have reviewed. My concerns are about the strength of several headline claims relative to the controls and statistics that support them, not about the value of the work.

## 2. Major concerns

**M1. The feedforward gain is estimated by reverse regression, which biases the open-loop baseline toward undershoot, and the claim that the comparison is insensitive to this is incorrect.**
*Where:* p. 19, Eq. (9) and lines 538-544; p. 10, lines 271-273; Table 6 (p. 30).
*Why it matters:* K_r is obtained by regressing stimulus amplitude on measured output. Under measurement noise this is the attenuated direction: the reverse-regression coefficient is systematically smaller in magnitude than the reciprocal of the forward dose-response slope, so the nominal open-loop command systematically under-drives and open loop sits short of the reference. The authors' own decomposition shows this is where most of the advantage lies - the standing offset supplies a median 35 % of open-loop residual energy versus 7 % in closed loop (p = 9.8e-4), while the genuinely fluctuation-related term falls only from 0.66 to 0.54 of disturbance energy (p = 0.0024; Table 6). The statement at lines 543-544 that sharing K_r makes the comparison independent of its value is not right: the size of the closed-minus-open gap depends directly on how well K_r is calibrated. With a perfectly calibrated constant input, most of the reported 23-32 % RMSE advantage would disappear.
*What would resolve it:* (a) Report a third, offline "best fixed input" baseline: rescale each session's open-loop command by the single scalar that minimizes that session's mean RMSE, and recompute the ratios of Table 4. This requires no new experiments. (b) Reframe the headline result around the fluctuation term (0.66 -> 0.54), which is the part attributable to feedback rather than to calibration. (c) Delete or correct the sentence at lines 543-544.

**M2. The gain that sets the feedforward term was fitted on a different signal, with a different normalization, from the one the controller regulates.**
*Where:* p. 17, lines 462-467; p. 18, lines 488-502; p. 19, lines 538-543.
*Why it matters:* The impulse analysis of Fig. 2 (from which Eq. 9 draws K_r) uses the hemodynamically corrected movie normalized by a fixed whole-session denominator, whereas every closed-loop analysis uses the uncorrected blue-channel signal normalized by a running 40 s baseline. A scalar gain is not invariant to either change. This is a plausible mechanical source of exactly the standing offset that the integrator then removes, which in turn is the dominant component of the reported closed-loop advantage (M1). It also undercuts the claim that the paper demonstrates a transferable procedure - the model is identified in one signal space and deployed in another.
*What would resolve it:* Re-derive K_r in the online normalization (the authors state at lines 493-495 that the online signal was re-extracted offline and matched the log at r = 0.995, so this is tractable), and report how much the gain changes. Separately, repeat the Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 analyses on the hemodynamically corrected offline signal and show that the conclusions are unchanged.

**M3. There is no opsin-negative, light-only, or sham condition, so the regulated quantity is not shown to be neural.**
*Where:* p. 16, lines 420-422 and 424-427 (acknowledged by the authors); abstract line 5; significance statement lines 18-21.
*Why it matters:* The loop feeds back on uncorrected fluorescence. A 638 nm laser delivered through the imaging objective can produce optical and hemodynamic changes in the green channel independent of opsin activation, and these would be absorbed into the identified plant and into the regulated variable. The abstract claims steering of *cortical activity*; what is demonstrated directly is regulation of a fluorescence signal. The authors are admirably explicit about this in Discussion, but the abstract, significance statement and Results are not.
*What would resolve it:* An opsin-negative control session (even one or two animals) with the same stimulation schedule would settle it. Failing that, two cheaper substitutes: (i) show the closed-loop results on the hemodynamically corrected offline signal (see M2); (ii) show that the spatial profile of the regulated suppression matches the opsin expression/inhibition profile rather than the laser spot profile - Fig. S1A,B is the right panel to extend. At minimum, qualify the abstract and significance statement to say that the controlled variable is uncorrected regional dF/F.

**M4. The headline state-dependence result is driven by the one state measure the authors themselves identify as confounded by condition, and it is measured over a window the controller shapes.**
*Where:* p. 8, lines 216-219 and 247-248; p. 24, lines 685-698; Fig. 4C,D; Table 5 (p. 30).
*Why it matters:* Absolute 1-4 Hz power for Fig. 4 is taken over [-2, +3] s, i.e. it spans the stimulation window. At lines 685-687 the authors state that an absolute spectral measure co-varies with signal magnitude, which differs between conditions by construction - which is precisely why they introduced the relative measure. The main conclusions of this section (absolute power has the largest unique R^2 for steady-state error, Fig. 4C; attenuation worsens by 8 % per SD, p = 0.0014, Table 5) then rest on that confounded measure, while the relative measure, which was designed to avoid the confound, shows effects that do not survive correction (lines 246-247). The caveat at lines 247-248 is one sentence and is not carried into the abstract (lines 10-11) or Discussion.
*What would resolve it:* Repeat the Fig. 4D / Table 5 analysis with all state variables computed on a strictly pre-stimulus window (e.g. [-2, 0) s), which is the window already used for Fig. 2G, and report it as the primary analysis. If the absolute-power effects survive on pre-stimulus state, the claim is secure; if they do not, the claim should be restricted to the relative measure or stated as concurrent association only.

**M5. Actuator saturation is an untested alternative explanation for the state dependence.**
*Where:* p. 20, lines 548-554; p. 15, lines 394-396; p. 23, lines 674-675.
*Why it matters:* The command is one-sided and clamped at u_max ~ 1 mW in most sessions. The authors note that large slow excursions may saturate it and that a time-at-ceiling analysis would separate delay from saturation, but do not perform it. The two sessions excluded from the disturbance analysis are ones in which the closed-loop mean sat 1.4-2.2 %dF/F *above* the reference - direct evidence of insufficient control authority in at least some sessions. If the command saturates more often in high absolute-delta-power trials, then "controller performance degrades with slow-wave activity" reduces to "the laser ran out of range", which is an instrument statement, not a brain-state statement.
*What would resolve it:* The logged commands already exist. Report, per state quartile and per session, the fraction of closed-loop samples at u_max (and at the proportional/integral clamps p = 5 V, q = 1 V of Eq. 10), and repeat the Table 5 attenuation tests restricted to non-saturating trials.

**M6. Animal-level inference is weak: 13 of 15 closed-loop sessions come from two mice, and the mouse-nested disturbance result includes no rejection.**
*Where:* p. 17, Table 1 and caption; Table S1 (p. 36); p. 29, Table 4; p. 30, Table 6; p. 27, lines 798-805 and 822-827.
*Why it matters:* Of the 15 sessions, 9 are Mouse 1 and 4 are Mouse 2; Mice 3 and 4 contribute one session each. In Table 4 the transient and steady-state tests report df = 14, i.e. the mouse variance was estimated at or near zero, so the random intercept is doing nothing and the effective replication is session-level within two animals. For the full 0-3 s window - the window from which the abstract's "all 15 sessions" / 23 % claim is drawn (abstract line 9; lines 195-196) - the degrees of freedom collapse to 1 and p = 0.089, and the authors state in a footnote that the model is not used for inference there. In Table 6 the mouse-nested rejection ratio is 0.57 with 95 % CI [0.22, 1.50], which includes one; the abstract (lines 11-12) nonetheless asserts that feedback rejects the disturbance without that qualification.
*What would resolve it:* (a) Make the transient and steady-state windows the only windows from which quantitative headline claims are drawn, and drop the 23 % figure from the abstract or attach its p-value. (b) State in the abstract and Results that the animal-level interval for the rejection ratio includes one. (c) Report the sensitivity of Table 4 to excluding Mouse 1, or to a per-mouse summary (4 points), so the reader can see how much rests on one animal. (d) Additional animals would of course be the strongest fix, but (a)-(c) can be done with the existing data.

**M7. Time-invariance is asserted but tested only against random split-halves, and the worst cross-session transfer is between two sessions of the same mouse on the same day.**
*Where:* abstract lines 6-7; p. 4, lines 115-128; Fig. 2E; p. 22, lines 624-629.
*Why it matters:* Held-out validation uses 100 *random* splits of trials within amplitude (lines 624-625). A random split is insensitive to drift in time, which is exactly what time-invariance within a session means. Across sessions, R^2 ranges from 0.97 to 0.12 (lines 125-126), and inspection of Fig. 2E shows that the 0.12 and 0.39 entries are the M5a <-> M5b pair - two sessions of one animal recorded on one day (Table 2, p. 28). This is the most informative number in the panel for the time-invariance claim and it is not discussed. Gains had to be recalibrated every session (lines 126-127), which is the honest consequence, but the abstract still advertises time-invariant dynamics.
*What would resolve it:* (a) Add a chronological split (first half versus second half of trials in acquisition order) alongside the random split in Fig. 2C,D. (b) Call out the same-mouse, same-day transfer failure explicitly in the text and say what it implies for the procedure. (c) Change the abstract to "approximately linear and, within a session, time-invariant", which is what the data support.

**M8. The MPC result is a self-consistent simulation in which the plant and the controller share a model, and the abstract attributes the gain to a capability the paper's own analysis shows is small.**
*Where:* p. 13, Fig. 6 legend; p. 12, lines 299-323; p. 21, lines 588-595; p. 15, lines 403-413; abstract line 14; Fig. S5E (p. 39) and Fig. 6H (p. 13).
*Why it matters:* Two separate problems. First, the replay drives a VARX fitted to the whole session with that trial's recorded one-step residuals (lines 588-591), so the simulated plant is in the MPC's own model class and responds identically in every brain state. The authors state this bounds rather than measures live performance (lines 411-413), but the abstract and significance statement do not. Second, the abstract credits "a cortical forecasting model" with the ~40 % improvement, while Fig. 6H and Fig. S5E show that at a 200 ms lead the 100 cortical sites raise R^2 only from 0.37 (site + laser) to 0.41. The gain is therefore attributable almost entirely to the plant model and constraint handling, not to forecasting the rest of cortex.
*What would resolve it:* (a) Say "in simulation" in the abstract and significance statement. (b) Re-run the replay against a plant that is *not* in the MPC's model class - a VARX of different order/history, a model fitted on a disjoint fold, or (best) a simple static nonlinearity in series - and report the degradation; this is a small amount of additional computation. (c) Rewrite the abstract sentence so the credited mechanism matches Fig. S5E, e.g. an MPC using a linear plant model with a one-second horizon.

**M9. The contralateral "disturbance" estimate is contaminated by the control input, and the session exclusion for this analysis was chosen after inspecting the data.**
*Where:* p. 10, lines 253-273; p. 26, lines 756-778, Eq. (19); p. 23, lines 670-677.
*Why it matters:* The predictor is fitted on laser-free frames, but the contralateral hemisphere itself dips during stimulation by a median 23 % of the readout's dip (lines 260-261, 770-771). The correction subtracts the session's *average* open-loop leak; any trial-to-trial variation in the leak remains in D and is correlated with the controller's action, which biases RR in the direction of apparent rejection. Separately, two of the 13 sessions were removed using a one-third standing-offset threshold chosen after looking at the sessions (lines 676-677) - the authors are candid, and note that any threshold between 20 % and 37 % selects the same eleven, but a post hoc criterion applied to the analysis it serves should be accompanied by the unfiltered result.
*What would resolve it:* (a) Validate the leak correction: show that the residual trial-wise correlation between the corrected D and the delivered command is near zero, or estimate the leak as a function of amplitude from open-loop trials at multiple powers rather than as a session mean. (b) Report RR for all 13 sessions alongside the 11, and report the version in which D is constructed only from pre-onset contralateral activity. (c) State in the Results, not only in the caption, that two sessions were removed and why.

**M10. The variance comparison between conditions is confounded by a difference in mean signal level.**
*Where:* p. 7, lines 203-214; Fig. 3D,E,I; Table 4, across-trial variance rows.
*Why it matters:* Closed loop and open loop differ systematically in mean dF/F during stimulation (that is the whole point of M1 - open loop carries a standing offset). Calcium fluorescence variance is level-dependent, so part of the 35-46 % variance reduction could be a consequence of operating at a different mean, not of disturbance rejection. The pre- and post-stimulation ratios near one (1.04, 1.05) show the effect is confined to the stimulation period, but that is also when the mean levels differ.
*What would resolve it:* Compare variance between conditions after matching for mean level - e.g. regress per-trial log variance on per-trial mean suppression and compare conditions at a common mean, or subsample open- and closed-loop trials with overlapping mean levels. Alternatively report variance of the detrended residual about each trial's own mean.

**M11. Two sessions enter the closed-loop comparison without the impulse characterization the procedure requires, and one session pools three different controllers.**
*Where:* p. 17, Table 1 caption (impulse sessions 1b, 3c, 5a, 5b); p. 23, lines 660-662; Table S1 note (p. 36); p. 6, lines 181-182.
*Why it matters:* The paper's central procedural claim (lines 77-80, 429-434) is: characterize the plant, check monotonicity, then tune PI with feedforward. But Mouse 2 (4 sessions) and Mouse 4 (1 session) have no impulse session, so Eq. (9) cannot have supplied their initial K_r. How was it set? This is not a pedantic point: it is the one step the paper asks other labs to adopt. Separately, Results state that the validation block ran with fixed gains (lines 181-182), while the Table S1 note says session 1e interleaved three K_p values - so one of the 15 "sessions" is three controllers pooled.
*What would resolve it:* State explicitly how K_r was initialized for mice without impulse data, and whether the procedure in fact degrades to hand tuning. Either exclude session 1e from the primary comparison or show that the result is unchanged when it is dropped (and report its three gain settings separately).

**M12. Internal numerical inconsistencies in Table 4.**
*Where:* Table 4, p. 29, pre-stimulation rows.
*Why it matters:* These are checkable arithmetic problems in the primary statistical table. (i) Pre-stimulation across-trial variance: the estimate is 1.04 (a 4 % *increase*) but the test statistic is reported as t(8.8) = -0.03. A ratio above one on the log scale cannot give a negative t for that model; and the reported CI [0.91, 1.18] implies |t| ~ 0.7, not 0.03. (ii) Pre-stimulation tracking RMSE: ratio 1.01 with CI [1.00, 1.03] and t(2.7) = 0.22. Back-computing, the reported CI half-width corresponds to |t| ~ 2.3 at df = 2.7, an order of magnitude from the stated statistic; the stated p = 0.84 is consistent with t = 0.22 but not with the CI. For comparison, I checked the 0-1 s and 1-3 s rows of Table 4 and all rows of Tables 5 and 6, and they are internally consistent, so this looks like a localized error rather than a systematic one.
*What would resolve it:* Recompute those two rows and state which quantity the CI is derived from (model SE with capped df, or something else), since the capped-df convention is described at lines 789-790 but its effect on the intervals is not.

## 3. Minor concerns

**m1. Truncated sentence.** p. 13, lines 324-325: the paragraph closing the MPC section ends mid-sentence. Complete or delete it.

**m2. Repository placeholder.** p. 27, lines 835-837: the code URL is an unfilled placeholder, processed data are promised only on acceptance and raw data only on request. For a paper whose contribution is a procedure and a rig, the acquisition and control software should be available to reviewers now. Please deposit at least the controller and analysis code at review.

**m3. Loop latency is not the binding delay.** p. 3, line 101 and p. 15, lines 391-393: the ~2.6-4 Hz bandwidth estimate is built from electronic latency plus the half-sample hold, but the dominant lag at this timescale is indicator and opsin kinetics (the paper's own dip time constant is ~0.113 s, lines 131-132). The Discussion half-acknowledges this (line 394) but the Results sentence at line 101 attributes the bandwidth limit to loop delay alone. Please state the combined limit where the claim is first made.

**m4. Latency distribution sample size.** Fig. 1F is based on 200 loop cycles. Worst-case latency is what matters for stability, and 200 cycles is ~6 s of operation. Report the distribution over a full session, or at least the maximum observed across sessions.

**m5. Few trials in some within-session regressions.** p. 25, lines 713-714 and Table S1: the error decomposition fits four-predictor within-session models, and Table S1 shows sessions with as few as 16 closed-loop trials (session 1h). Adjusted R^2 at n = 16 with p = 4 is very noisy and can go negative. Report per-session trial counts in the Fig. 4C legend, and show the decomposition weighted by trials or restricted to sessions above a threshold.

**m6. Asymmetric post-stimulation windows.** Fig. 3H uses 1 s after offset while Fig. 3I uses 3 s (legend, p. 7), and Table 4 reports both under one heading. Justify the asymmetry or use a common window.

**m7. The integral term is reset every trial.** p. 20, lines 550-552: the loop is only ever run over 3 s transients with the integrator reset between trials, yet the Discussion invokes asymptotic results - the internal model principle (Francis and Wonham, lines 371-372) and low-gain integral stability for exponentially stable plants (Logemann and Curtain, lines 373-375) - which concern steady-state behaviour of a non-resetting loop. The mapping from those theorems to a 3 s reset-integral controller should be made explicit, or the appeals softened. The authors' own justification for not reporting gain and phase margins (lines 555-559) is reasonable and could be extended to cover this.

**m8. Monotonicity claim rests on a narrow, one-sided operating range.** p. 4, lines 111-114: three to nine amplitudes up to 1.6 mW, one session with R^2 = 0.73, and inhibition only. "Approximately linear" (abstract line 6) should be qualified by the tested range, and the gain spread across sessions (3.5-fold, -0.88 to -3.11) should appear in the abstract or at least in the significance statement, since it is what forces per-session recalibration.

**m9. "Well predicted" overstates the contralateral predictor.** Significance statement line 23; p. 10, line 256: held-out R^2 ranges 0.44-0.98 with median 0.76. At the low end, more than half the variance of the controlled site is unexplained, and that is the end where the disturbance estimate matters most. Soften, or report RR against predictor quality.

**m10. The controlled site is adjacent to, not at, the stimulation spot.** p. 3, lines 93-95; p. 16, lines 422-423. The abstract and significance statement describe control of "a cortical region" without saying that the readout kernel is a 0.173 mm box next to the laser spot and that the regulation depends on spread of inhibition. One clause in the abstract would fix this.

**m11. One-sided actuation should be stated earlier.** The system can only suppress; references are always below baseline. This is acknowledged in Discussion (lines 384-385) but "steer cortical activity" (abstract line 5) and "toward a desired reference" (line 18) read as bidirectional.

**m12. Fig. 5 generalization.** Three sessions in one mouse, no statistics (stated honestly at line 292). The abstract (lines 12-13) presents the preview result without the n. Add "in one mouse" to the abstract sentence.

**m13. Figure legibility.** Fig. 1A-F is too small to read at print size; the block diagram in Fig. 1E is illegible, as are the axis labels in Fig. 1F (only the two endpoints are marked). The session labels on the x-axis of Fig. 4G cannot be read, which matters because the reader should be able to check them against the two sessions excluded at line 674. Fig. S4A traces are too small to convey anything. Please enlarge or split.

**m14. Fig. 2B legend trial counts.** The legend merges Mice 5a and 5b as 44-59 trials per amplitude, while Table 2 gives 44-59 and 45-58 separately. Trivial, but the legend and table should agree.

**m15. Reuse of the value 0.56.** Fig. 2C labels a per-amplitude held-out R^2 of 0.56 in Mouse 1, while line 118 reports a per-session median of 0.56 in Mouse 5a. Same number, different quantity, same page - easy to misread.

**m16. Laser power calibration.** p. 17, lines 475-477: a single conversion (1 mW per 3 V) is used for the impulse and all 2025 sessions, with the nearest-in-time calibration used only in 2026. Given that reported amplitudes in mW underpin the dose-response slopes of Fig. 2B, please state the drift observed between calibrations, or report amplitudes in command volts with mW as a secondary axis.

**m17. Mouse numbering is not in acquisition order.** Table 1 (p. 17): Mouse 5 was recorded 2025-12-02, before Mice 3 and 4 (2026). The caption states that *session letters* run in date order, which is helpful, but mouse numbering running against date is a small avoidable source of confusion in Tables 2 and S1.

**m18. Table 3 motion result.** The text reports the motion effect as a finding (lines 154-157), but its supporting test is p = 0.030 against the stated Bonferroni threshold of 0.017 (Table 3). The text already describes it as a drop at the highest quartile rather than a graded trend; please also state that it does not clear the threshold.

**m19. Sex is reported but not analysed.** p. 26, line 782 (2 male, 2 female) and Table 1. With 4 animals no sex comparison is possible; a sentence saying so would satisfy reporting guidelines.

**m20. Reliance on an unpublished methods source.** The preparation, rig, mesoscope and laser calibration are all deferred to Matveev et al. (2024), listed as a bioRxiv preprint (p. 32, lines 909-910). Several claims a reader would want to check - that the 470/405 nm illumination does not activate the opsin (line 461), the power calibration at the focal plane, the atlas registration - are not verifiable from this manuscript. Please include the critical parameters here, or note the publication status of that reference in the text.

**m21. Reference formatting.** Gupta and Murphy is given as eLife 14:RP105070 with year 2026 (p. 31, lines 873-874); eLife volume 14 corresponds to 2025 and RP numbers denote Reviewed Preprints, whose citation form differs. Please check the year and version-of-record status of this and the other 2026 entries.

## 4. Overall recommendation

**Major revision.**

The engineering is real, the problem is well chosen, and the paper is unusually honest about its own limits - the Discussion (pp. 15-16, lines 388-441) pre-empts many of the objections above, and the Materials and Methods are exemplary in detail. The core demonstration, that a PI controller with feedforward can hold a mesoscale cortical readout near a reference better than a fixed input, is likely to survive scrutiny. But three of the paper's four headline claims are currently supported more weakly than the abstract implies, and in each case the fix is available within the existing dataset:

- The closed-versus-open-loop advantage is dominated by removal of a calibration offset that the chosen estimator for K_r predictably produces (M1, M2), and the claim that the comparison is independent of K_r is wrong as written.
- The brain-state result rests on a measure the authors themselves flag as condition-confounded, computed over a window the controller shapes, with actuator saturation untested as an alternative (M4, M5).
- The MPC result is a simulation whose plant shares the controller's model class, and the mechanism credited in the abstract is contradicted by the paper's own Fig. S5E (M8).

Adding to that the thin animal-level replication (M6), the un-addressed possibility that the regulated signal is partly non-neural (M3), and two arithmetic inconsistencies in the primary statistical table (M12), I do not think the paper can be accepted in its present form. None of this requires new animals. The decisive additions are: an offline best-fixed-input baseline; the state analysis repeated on a strictly pre-stimulus window; a saturation audit from the logged commands; the closed-loop analyses repeated on the hemodynamically corrected signal; an MPC replay against an out-of-class plant; and corrected abstract claims with their n's and caveats attached. With those, I would expect to recommend acceptance.

I would be glad to review a revised version.
